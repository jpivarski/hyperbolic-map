Infinite dungeon

The same dungeon, split into an atlas. Every cell of the tiling is its own little coordinate system, so the numbers describing a room are always small no matter how far from the origin that room is. Wander as far as you like — or zoom out until the whole infinite world fits in the disk, where a world-turtle is carrying it.

Three sources, composed in one callback

The callback that supplies each tile merges three things, which is the point of returning data rather than a URL:

  1. a prototype room — seven shapes, stored once, drawn in every cell;
  2. video-game characters — nine of them, each stored once in generic cell-local coordinates, then scattered through infinitely many rooms by the hash below;
  3. room numbers — there are infinitely many, so they are computed on the fly from the cell’s own coordinates.

An infinite random dungeon from one random number. Three rooms in four are empty; the rest hold a character — Mr. T with probability 1/30 and the other seven sharing the remaining 29/30 evenly, so he turns up about a quarter as often as any of them. That cannot be decided in advance, because there is no end to the rooms — and it cannot be decided lazily and remembered, because you would be storing a growing list forever. So nothing is remembered: one 64-bit salt is drawn at page load, and each room’s contents are a pure function of that salt and the room’s own (latitude, longitude). Walk away and come back and the room is unchanged, however deep, because it was never stored in the first place — it is recomputed. Reload the page for an entirely different dungeon.

Two details that matter. The hash is BigInt arithmetic, not floating point: addresses are exact at any depth, and truncating them to a double would make distant rooms alias onto each other. And it is a real mixing function (splitmix64’s finaliser), because neighbouring rooms have addresses differing by one — a weak hash puts visible stripes of the same character along a row. Measured over 32,000 neighbour pairs, adjacent rooms match 57.3 % of the time against 57.1 % expected for independent draws.

The turtle and the star field are not library features. They are two image layers defined in demo/layers.js: one turns with the disk, one does not. Turning with the disk means something slightly different here than in a single-patch view: the view matrix is expressed in the anchor tile’s frame, and the anchor changes as you walk, so the raw rotation jumps by as much as 34° at a cell boundary that the dungeon itself crosses perfectly smoothly. The layer cancels those jumps. What it cannot do is pin the shell rigidly to the plane — that needs the global frame this whole page exists to avoid.

Clipping is off here, deliberately. The 2012 room art is drawn straddling cell boundaries — the floor plate spans the corner where four cells meet, and the doors are what connect adjacent rooms, so they have to cross the boundary. Confining each cell’s drawing to its own cell would sever every junction and leave fragments. The Escher tile, whose art is designed to fill its tile, clips correctly — which is why the library makes this a per-atlas choice rather than a rule.

Unclipped art makes the order the cells are drawn in matter, because neighbours overlap and whichever paints last wins the seam. The atlas walks outward from the camera, which would make that order depend on where you are standing — pan a little and a hidden seam surfaces as a stray stroke across a floor plate. So this page pins the order to the cell addresses instead, with drawOrder: "V>>": latitude first ascending, then longitude ascending. All eight codes are legal geometry; only this one draws the picture the 2012 art intends. Addresses are exact integers at any depth, so the order never wavers however far you walk.

Why the numbers are always small: cell (latitude, longitude) is x ∈ [lon·2lat, (lon+1)·2lat], y ∈ [2lat, 2lat+1] in the half-plane — but in its own frame every cell is the identical box x ∈ ±1/(2√2), y ∈ [2−½, 2½]. The 2lat divides out. In the single-patch version the far rooms need coordinates up to 11712, where a disk coordinate has only about seven significant digits left.
